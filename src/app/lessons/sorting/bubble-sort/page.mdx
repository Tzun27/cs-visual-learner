# Bubble Sort

**Difficulty:** beginner · **Category:** sorting

Bubble sort walks the array from left to right, comparing each pair of adjacent elements and
swapping them when they're out of order. Each full pass guarantees that the largest still-unsorted
value settles into its final position at the end. After $n - 1$ passes, the array is sorted.

<SortingViz algorithm="bubble" initialSize={16} />

## What you're seeing

- **Yellow** bars are being _compared_.
- **Blue** bars (with a tick mark) just _swapped_.
- **Green** bars are in their _final sorted position_.

Step forward and watch the largest value drift right one position per swap. Each outer pass shortens
the unsorted prefix by one — that's why the algorithm makes fewer comparisons toward the end.

## Why is it $O(n^2)$?

Each pass through the array makes at most $n - 1$ comparisons. We need up to $n - 1$ passes to
guarantee everything is sorted. So the total comparisons are bounded by

$$
(n-1) + (n-2) + \cdots + 1 = \tfrac{n(n-1)}{2} = \Theta(n^2).
$$

Try increasing the array size and play it again. You'll feel the quadratic growth — doubling the
size roughly _quadruples_ the number of comparisons.

## When is it actually useful?

Almost never in practice — but two cases matter for intuition:

1. **Already-sorted input.** A small optimization (stop early when no swap occurred during a pass)
   makes bubble sort run in $O(n)$ on already-sorted data.
2. **Tiny arrays.** With fewer than ~10 elements, the constant-factor overhead of fancier
   algorithms can lose to bubble sort. This is why some library implementations of `quicksort`
   fall back to an $O(n^2)$ sort below a threshold.

The real value of bubble sort is pedagogical: it gives you an intuitive picture of "comparing
adjacent pairs," which is the foundation for understanding more sophisticated sorts.
