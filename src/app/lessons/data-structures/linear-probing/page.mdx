# Hash Tables: Linear Probing

**Difficulty:** intermediate · **Category:** data structures

The [previous hash-table lesson](/lessons/data-structures/hash-tables) used **separate chaining** —
when two keys collide, the second one becomes part of a linked list hanging off the bucket. The
table is an array of variable-length chains, and deletes are a simple list splice.

This lesson visualizes the other major strategy: **open addressing**, specifically **linear
probing**. There is exactly one slot per array index. Every key lives directly in the table, no
auxiliary structures. When a collision happens, the colliding key walks forward — one slot at a
time, wrapping around the end — until it finds an empty seat.

The motivation is cache locality. A chain is great for theory and writability, but each pointer
hop is potentially a cache miss; a linear-probing table fits its values in contiguous memory and
gets to ride the CPU's prefetcher. Python's `dict`, Rust's `HashMap`, and the JVM's
`open-addressing` hash sets all use variants of this approach for that reason.

## Inserting into an open-addressed table

<LinearProbeInsertViz />

Each cell shows one slot. Empty slots are drawn dashed; occupied slots show their key; we'll
introduce the third state (tombstone) in the next section.

Watch the **Probes** counter as the viz advances. The first three inserts pile into the same
neighborhood because they all hash to slot 5 (each value is $5 \bmod 8 = 5$ or $13 \bmod 8 = 5$ or
$21 \bmod 8 = 5$). The second one probes once and lands at slot 6; the third probes twice and
lands at slot 7. This is **primary clustering** — once a few neighboring slots fill up, every new
key that hashes anywhere into that region has to walk all the way past the cluster.

A couple of paths worth pausing on:

- **Wrap-around.** The fifth insert is $23$, which hashes to slot $7$. Slot 7 is already taken by
  $21$, so the probe walks forward — and wraps to slot 0. Linear probing treats the table as a
  ring; the probe sequence is $i, (i+1) \bmod c, (i+2) \bmod c, \ldots$.
- **Duplicate detection.** The final insert is $5$ a second time. The probe walks to slot $5$,
  sees an exact match, and aborts without making a new entry. Compared to separate chaining,
  duplicate detection is the same idea: walk the probe sequence and check each existing key.

The amortized cost of insert is $O(1)$ — _but only if the table stays sparsely loaded_. Once the
**load factor** $\alpha = \frac{\text{occupied}}{\text{capacity}}$ climbs above about $0.7$,
primary clustering makes probe sequences grow rapidly, and production implementations resize the
backing array (typically doubling it) to keep $\alpha$ bounded. This lesson sticks with a fixed
capacity of 8 so the viz stays legible.

## Searching with tombstones

Linear-probing _delete_ is where the design has to get clever — and that cleverness shapes how
**search** works too. Let's start with search.

<LinearProbeSearchViz />

The starting table for this section is the same as the insert section's end state, except we've
already deleted $13$ to plant a tombstone at slot 6. (Tombstones are drawn as cells with a
small × through them.)

The search algorithm is structurally identical to insert's probing: hash to the home slot, then
walk forward until you find your key, or run out. The decision is what counts as "running out":

- **Empty slot → stop, declare miss.** An empty slot at index $i$ means nothing has ever lived at
  $i$ during the lifetime of this table. So if the key we're searching for were here, the original
  insert would have placed it at $i$ — and since it's not at $i$, it's not in the table at all.
- **Tombstone slot → keep going.** A tombstone at $i$ means a key _used to_ live at $i$ and got
  deleted. Critically, _other keys_ might have arrived later and probed past $i$ on their way to a
  slot further down. So we have to keep walking.

Watch the second search, for $21$. The probe path is $5 \to 6 \to 7$:

1. Slot 5 is occupied by $5$ (not $21$) → probe.
2. Slot 6 is a **tombstone** → probe (do not stop here!).
3. Slot 7 has $21$ → found.

If we had stopped at the tombstone, we would have falsely reported $21$ as missing. That's the
whole point of tombstones: they preserve the "someone probed through here" history.

The third search, for $13$ (the key we deleted), is the contrasting case. The probe walks
$5 \to 6 \to 7 \to 0$ — past the tombstone, past every key in the cluster, and finally to a
genuinely empty slot at index 0. Empty means "not here," so it's a miss.

## Deleting: why we can't just clear the slot

Now the delete operation itself becomes inevitable.

<LinearProbeDeleteViz />

The delete algorithm walks the probe sequence the same way search does, finds the matching slot,
and replaces it with a **tombstone** — not with `EMPTY`. That single design choice is what makes
the search logic from the previous section work.

Trace the first delete in the viz, for $13$:

1. Hash to slot 5. Probe past 5 (the key $5$, not $13$).
2. Slot 6 has $13$ — found.
3. Replace slot 6 with a tombstone.

The slot's contents are visibly gone, but the slot itself isn't reset to empty. Any future search
that probes through index 6 will correctly walk past it.

The second delete, for $4$, is the simple case: $4$ lives at its home slot, no probing, tombstoned
in one step. The third target, $99$, isn't in the table at all — its home slot ($99 \bmod 8 = 3$)
is empty, so the search half of delete reports a miss immediately, and nothing changes.

## The cost of tombstones

Tombstones make linear-probing _correct_, but they don't come free:

- **Searches and inserts have to walk through them.** A table that has accumulated tombstones
  behaves like one with a higher effective load factor — even though `liveKeys / capacity` is
  low, probe sequences can still be long.
- **The table can grow "logically full" while half empty.** If you alternate inserts and deletes
  on a small enough capacity, the entire probe range can be tombstones with no real keys in it.
  Insert performance degrades catastrophically.

The fix is **rehashing**: when the count of tombstones gets high enough, allocate a fresh backing
array of the same (or larger) capacity and reinsert only the live keys. Tombstones evaporate.
Real implementations interleave this with their normal resizing strategy — for instance, Python's
`dict` does a resize-in-place when tombstones reach about ⅔ of the capacity.

## Linear probing vs. separate chaining

Both strategies handle collisions, both are $O(1)$ amortized for the standard operations. They
just trade off in different ways:

|                  | Separate chaining (chained buckets)                         | Linear probing (open addressing)                                    |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Memory layout    | Array of pointers + scattered chain nodes                   | Single contiguous array                                             |
| Cache behavior   | Pointer chase per probe — possible cache miss               | Adjacent slots; CPU prefetcher helps                                |
| Worst-case load  | Degrades gracefully — chains just grow longer               | Degrades sharply past $\alpha \approx 0.7$ due to clustering        |
| Delete           | Simple list splice                                          | Must leave a tombstone                                              |
| Resizing trigger | Average chain length                                        | Load factor; tombstone count                                        |
| Best at          | Unpredictable load, frequent deletes, large values per slot | Numeric or small-value keys, predictable load, read-heavy workloads |

In modern hardware-aware design, the cache argument tends to win for typical workloads — which is
why so many production hash tables use a variant of open addressing. But primary clustering is a
real failure mode, and the data structure has to compensate (Robin Hood probing, double hashing,
or fancier schemes like Hopscotch) when the trade-off bites.

## Robin Hood probing: equalizing displacement

Plain linear probing has a problem you can see in the insert section above: some keys end up
with displacement 0 (lucky home placement), others get pushed 2 or 3 slots away. The variance is
high. And under a random hash function, that variance grows roughly as $O(\log n)$ — the worst
probe length is much higher than the average.

**Robin Hood probing** is a small change to insert that flattens this distribution. The rule:
each key tracks how far it has probed from its home; when probing into an occupied slot, compare
displacements. If the inserting key has probed _further_ than the slot's current resident, swap —
the inserting key takes the slot, and the (now-evicted) resident continues probing forward with
its own displacement counter. "Rob from the rich, give to the poor."

<RobinHoodInsertViz />

The viz uses a slightly different curated input: $[5, 14, 13, 22]$. The interesting step is the
third insert. Trace it:

1. $13$ hashes to slot 5. Slot 5 holds $5$ (displacement 0). $13$'s probe count is 0 too;
   0 > 0 is false, so no swap. Advance to slot 6, probe count becomes 1.
2. Slot 6 holds $14$ with displacement 0 (its home is slot 6). Now $13$'s probe count is 1 and
   $14$'s is 0; **1 > 0 is true → swap**.
3. $13$ takes slot 6 (with displacement 1). $14$ becomes the active key, continuing forward with
   its updated probe count of 1.
4. Advance to slot 7 (empty) and place $14$ there with displacement 1.

After all four inserts, both $13$ and $14$ sit at displacement 1. Without Robin Hood, $14$
would have stayed at slot 6 (displacement 0) and $13$ would have gone to slot 7 (displacement 2) —
a less even distribution. The end-state max-displacement is _smaller_ with Robin Hood, even
though the total probe count is similar.

The catch is that delete and search get more complex. Production Robin Hood implementations use
**backshift deletion** (rather than tombstones) — when removing a key, walk forward and pull each
subsequent key one slot to the left until you hit an empty slot or a key already at its home.
That keeps the displacement invariants tight, but it's more bookkeeping per delete. This section
focuses on insert because that's where the variance-reduction story lives.

## What's next

This lesson covered the simplest open-addressing scheme and Robin Hood as a variance-reducing
refinement. Other natural follow-ups:

- **Quadratic probing** changes the probe sequence from $i + 1, i + 2, i + 3, \ldots$ to
  $i + 1, i + 4, i + 9, \ldots$ — same wrap-around, but the gaps spread out, which breaks up
  primary clustering at the cost of needing capacity to be a prime so all slots get visited.
- **Double hashing** uses a second hash function to determine the step size, so different keys
  with the same home slot probe along different paths. Cleanest clustering avoidance, at the cost
  of a second hash computation per probe.
- **Backshift deletion** is the natural companion to Robin Hood probing: deleting a key by
  pulling subsequent keys one slot toward their homes, restoring the displacement invariants
  Robin Hood maintains on insert.
