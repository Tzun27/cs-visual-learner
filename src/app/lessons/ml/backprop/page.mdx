# Backpropagation

**Difficulty:** intermediate · **Category:** machine learning

<MathLevel />

<IntuitionOnly>

The [gradient-descent lesson](/lessons/ml/gradient-descent) showed how a learner walks
downhill in parameter space — but it skipped the hardest question. **How do we know which
direction is downhill for every weight in a network with hundreds of parameters?**

That's what **backpropagation** answers. It's a recipe for computing the gradient of the
loss with respect to every weight, by walking the network _backwards_ from the output and
multiplying together a few small numbers at each step.

The viz below runs one forward + one backward pass on a tiny network: two inputs feed two
hidden units (each with a ReLU activation), which feed a single output. The whole
calculation is exactly nine multiplications and a few additions — small enough to fit on
this page. Same recipe scales up to GPT-sized models with billions of parameters.

The mental model worth carrying away: each edge of the network gets "blamed" in proportion
to how much it contributed to the final error. A weight whose downstream connection is
already near zero gets a small share of the blame. A weight on the path that produced the
big wrong number gets a large share. That's the chain rule.

</IntuitionOnly>

<MathOnly>

Backpropagation is the chain rule applied to a directed computation graph. Given a
differentiable loss $L$ that depends on parameters $\mathbf{\theta} = (w_1, \ldots, w_n)$
through intermediate activations, we want $\partial L / \partial w_i$ for every $i$.

The recipe:

1. **Forward pass.** Compute every intermediate activation, top to bottom.
2. **Backward pass.** Starting from $\partial L / \partial \text{output} = $ trivial, work
   backwards through each operation in reverse topological order. For each intermediate
   $u$ whose immediate downstream dependents are $\{v_k\}$, apply
   $$
     \frac{\partial L}{\partial u} = \sum_k \frac{\partial L}{\partial v_k} \cdot \frac{\partial v_k}{\partial u}
   $$
3. **Weight update.** Each weight gets $w \leftarrow w - \alpha \cdot \partial L / \partial w$.

For the network in the viz — $\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ with ReLU
nonlinearity and MSE loss — the chain unfolds as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial y} &= y - t \\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial v_i} &= (y - t) \cdot h_i \\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_i^{\text{pre}}} &= (y - t) \cdot v_i \cdot \mathbb{1}[h_i^{\text{pre}} > 0] \\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{ij}} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_i^{\text{pre}}} \cdot x_j
\end{aligned}
$$

The indicator $\mathbb{1}[h_i^{\text{pre}} > 0]$ is the ReLU derivative. When the pre-activation
is negative, the gradient is exactly zero — the so-called "dead ReLU" problem. The unit
contributes nothing to the output, _and_ no update can revive it (the weights feeding it
won't budge, because their gradient is also zero).

The factor of $\frac{1}{2}$ in the loss $L = \frac{1}{2}(y - t)^2$ is a convention that makes
$\partial L / \partial y = y - t$ instead of $2(y - t)$ — same minimum, cleaner gradient.

</MathOnly>

<IntuitionOnly>

Walk it through with the **step-forward** button:

1. **Inputs arrive** at the network's left edge.
2. **Forward through $h_1$**: multiply each incoming weight by its input, add them up,
   pass through ReLU (which clips negatives to zero), get the hidden activation.
3. **Forward through $h_2$**: same recipe, different weights.
4. **Output**: weighted sum of $h_1, h_2$ plus the bias $c$.
5. **Loss**: half the squared error against the target.
6. **Backward at the output**: $\partial L / \partial y$ is just $y - t$ — the error itself.
   Then $\partial L / \partial v_1$ etc. fall out by chain rule.
7. **Backward through $h_1$**: the gradient that flowed into $h_1$ gets routed back to
   each of $h_1$'s incoming weights. The ReLU gate either lets it through (1) or blocks
   it (0).
8. **Backward through $h_2$**: same recipe.
9. **Apply update**: every weight steps in its descent direction by a small amount.

What's not in the viz: real networks are deeper (more layers) and wider (more units per
layer), but each layer's backward pass is _exactly the same recipe_. That's the magic —
the algorithm scales.

</IntuitionOnly>

<MathOnly>

What backprop _isn't_: it's not a special form of numerical differentiation. The recipe
is exact (up to floating-point error) — the gradients you see are the analytical ones,
not finite-difference approximations.

What backprop _is_: a particular evaluation order of the chain rule that **reuses
intermediate results**. The naive way to compute $\partial L / \partial w_{ij}$ for every
$w_{ij}$ separately requires recomputing the network output for each parameter, costing
$O(P)$ network evaluations for $P$ parameters. Backprop computes all $P$ gradients in
$O(1)$ extra forward+backward passes, total cost $O(P)$ — the same as one forward pass.
This is why training was historically intractable until backprop made it $O(P)$ instead
of $O(P^2)$.

If you've heard "automatic differentiation" (autodiff): backpropagation is the
reverse-mode flavor of autodiff. Modern ML libraries (PyTorch, JAX) implement autodiff
generically — you write the forward pass in straight code, and the framework derives the
backward pass automatically by symbolic chain-rule.

</MathOnly>

## Why ReLU and not sigmoid?

Old textbooks teach backprop with a sigmoid activation: $\sigma(x) = 1 / (1 + e^{-x})$.
ReLU was a quiet revolution somewhere around 2010 — most modern networks use it (or a
close cousin like GELU). Two reasons:

- **The gradient doesn't vanish.** Sigmoid's derivative is at most $1/4$, and it goes
  to zero for inputs far from zero. Multiply a few of those together through a deep
  network and the gradients flowing back from the output decay exponentially. ReLU's
  derivative is exactly $1$ in the active region — gradients propagate without decay.
- **It's cheaper.** $\max(0, x)$ is one comparison and a select. $\sigma(x)$ involves
  an exponential.

The cost of ReLU's simplicity is the dead-neuron problem: a unit whose pre-activation goes
negative across all training examples will never recover, because no gradient flows
through it. Variants like **Leaky ReLU** ($\max(0.01x, x)$) and **GELU** address this by
letting a small gradient through everywhere.
