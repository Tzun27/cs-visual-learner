# Attention

**Difficulty:** advanced · **Category:** machine learning

<MathLevel />

<IntuitionOnly>

Every modern language model — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini — is built out of the same primitive,
stacked dozens of times: the **attention head**. This lesson dismantles a single one of
them.

The mental model worth carrying away: each token in a sequence asks every other token
"how relevant are you to me?" and then takes a weighted average of their values, where
the weights are the relevance scores. The clever part is that "relevance" itself is
_learned_ — encoded as three separate projection matrices (Q, K, V) that the network can
tune during training.

Three roles per token:

- **Q (query):** what am I looking for? (asked by this token)
- **K (key):** what do I represent? (offered by every token, including this one)
- **V (value):** what do I contribute, if you decide to attend to me?

The softmax converts raw scores into proper weights — non-negative numbers that sum to
1 along each query's row. That's what makes the output a _weighted average_ (the dashed
box around the heatmap shows the current row sum staying at 1 as the values flow).

Three tokens is a toy example. Real attention layers work the same way with thousands of
tokens, but the picture wouldn't fit on a screen — the matrix would be a 4000 × 4000 grid.

</IntuitionOnly>

<MathOnly>

A **single-head scaled dot-product attention** layer takes a sequence of $n$ tokens
$X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{\text{embed}}}$ and produces a new sequence
$Y \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_v}$ via:

$$
Q = X W_Q, \quad K = X W_K, \quad V = X W_V
$$

$$
A = \text{softmax}\!\left( \frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right), \quad Y = A V
$$

where $W_Q, W_K \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{embed}} \times d_k}$ and $W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{embed}} \times d_v}$ are learned parameters.

Two design choices worth unpacking:

**Why $\sqrt{d_k}$?** When $Q$ and $K$ are initialized with components from a unit-variance
distribution, the dot product $Q_i \cdot K_j$ has variance $d_k$ — so its standard deviation
grows like $\sqrt{d_k}$. Without scaling, softmax of large scores becomes near-one-hot
(all weight on one entry), and gradients through the softmax saturate to zero. Dividing
by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps the pre-softmax scores in a similar range regardless of $d_k$, which
keeps gradients flowing.

**Why split into Q, K, V instead of just one matrix?** A single embedding can't usefully
play three different roles at once. Letting "what am I looking for" (Q) and "what do I
represent" (K) be different learned views of the same token gives the network expressive
power that vanilla embedding lookups can't match. V then carries _what the token actually
contributes_ when attended to — which can be different from either Q or K.

</MathOnly>

<AttentionViz />

<IntuitionOnly>

The demo uses three tokens labeled $t_1, t_2, t_3$ with the same fixed Q, K, V projection
matrices on every visit (so the trajectory is deterministic). Step through and notice:

1. **Embeddings X are the same input for all three projections** — they're fan-out.
2. **The 3×3 attention matrix** has each row sum to exactly 1 (the dashed box on the
   heatmap). Row $i$ is "where does token $i$'s query land?"
3. **Each output is a literal mixture** — Y[0] is some weighted blend of V[0], V[1], V[2]
   with weights that came from row 0 of A.

What multi-head attention adds (and what's NOT in this lesson): instead of one (Q, K, V)
triple, real models use 8–96 of them _in parallel_, each with its own learned matrices.
Each head can specialize on a different kind of relationship — one might track which
adjective modifies which noun, another might track which subject goes with which verb, and
so on. The outputs of all heads are concatenated and projected back down. Same mechanism,
applied many times.

</IntuitionOnly>

<MathOnly>

A few extensions that the viz deliberately omits, for context:

- **Causal masking** — at training time on next-token-prediction tasks, the score matrix
  is masked so position $i$ can only attend to positions $j \leq i$. Implemented as
  $S_{ij} \leftarrow -\infty$ for $j > i$ before softmax. This is what lets a transformer
  process the whole sequence in parallel during training while still predicting tokens
  causally at inference.
- **Multi-head attention** generalizes by running $h$ heads in parallel with their own
  $W_Q^{(k)}, W_K^{(k)}, W_V^{(k)}$ matrices, concatenating the per-head outputs, and
  projecting:
  $$
    \text{MHA}(X) = \text{concat}(Y^{(1)}, \ldots, Y^{(h)}) W_O
  $$
- **Positional information** has to be added to the embeddings explicitly (rotary
  embeddings, learned positional encodings, etc.) because raw attention is
  permutation-equivariant — it can't distinguish "the cat sat" from "sat the cat".

The KQV matrices in real transformers are usually low-rank decompositions:
$d_k = d_{\text{embed}} / h$ for $h$ heads. The total parameter count is the same as a
single head with $d_k = d_{\text{embed}}$, but spread across more independent attention
computations.

</MathOnly>

## What's the point?

If you've followed [gradient descent](/lessons/ml/gradient-descent) and
[backpropagation](/lessons/ml/backprop), you now have the full training loop for a
transformer in your head:

1. Stack a few dozen attention + feed-forward layers.
2. Pass a batch of token sequences through; compute a next-token-prediction loss.
3. Backpropagate gradients through every layer — the recipe doesn't change.
4. Apply one gradient-descent step to every weight.
5. Repeat.

That's it. There's no other secret — just lots of compute and lots of data. The "magic"
of large language models is the scale, not the algorithm. The mechanism inside this lesson
is the same one running in every transformer ever shipped.
