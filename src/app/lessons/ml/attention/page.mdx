# Attention

**Difficulty:** advanced · **Category:** machine learning

<MathLevel />

<IntuitionOnly>

Every modern language model — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini — is built out of the same primitive,
stacked dozens of times: the **attention head**. This lesson dismantles a single one of
them.

The mental model worth carrying away: each token in a sequence asks every other token
"how relevant are you to me?" and then takes a weighted average of their values, where
the weights are the relevance scores. The clever part is that "relevance" itself is
_learned_ — encoded as three separate projection matrices (Q, K, V) that the network can
tune during training.

Three roles per token:

- **Q (query):** what am I looking for? (asked by this token)
- **K (key):** what do I represent? (offered by every token, including this one)
- **V (value):** what do I contribute, if you decide to attend to me?

The softmax converts raw scores into proper weights — non-negative numbers that sum to
1 along each query's row. That's what makes the output a _weighted average_ (the dashed
box around the heatmap shows the current row sum staying at 1 as the values flow).

Three tokens is a toy example. Real attention layers work the same way with thousands of
tokens, but the picture wouldn't fit on a screen — the matrix would be a 4000 × 4000 grid.

</IntuitionOnly>

<MathOnly>

A **single-head scaled dot-product attention** layer takes a sequence of $n$ tokens
$X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{\text{embed}}}$ and produces a new sequence
$Y \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_v}$ via:

$$
Q = X W_Q, \quad K = X W_K, \quad V = X W_V
$$

$$
A = \text{softmax}\!\left( \frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right), \quad Y = A V
$$

where $W_Q, W_K \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{embed}} \times d_k}$ and $W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{embed}} \times d_v}$ are learned parameters.

Two design choices worth unpacking:

**Why $\sqrt{d_k}$?** When $Q$ and $K$ are initialized with components from a unit-variance
distribution, the dot product $Q_i \cdot K_j$ has variance $d_k$ — so its standard deviation
grows like $\sqrt{d_k}$. Without scaling, softmax of large scores becomes near-one-hot
(all weight on one entry), and gradients through the softmax saturate to zero. Dividing
by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps the pre-softmax scores in a similar range regardless of $d_k$, which
keeps gradients flowing.

**Why split into Q, K, V instead of just one matrix?** A single embedding can't usefully
play three different roles at once. Letting "what am I looking for" (Q) and "what do I
represent" (K) be different learned views of the same token gives the network expressive
power that vanilla embedding lookups can't match. V then carries _what the token actually
contributes_ when attended to — which can be different from either Q or K.

</MathOnly>

<AttentionViz />

<IntuitionOnly>

The demo uses three tokens labeled $t_1, t_2, t_3$ with the same fixed Q, K, V projection
matrices on every visit (so the trajectory is deterministic). Step through and notice:

1. **Embeddings X are the same input for all three projections** — they're fan-out.
2. **The 3×3 attention matrix** has each row sum to exactly 1 (the dashed box on the
   heatmap). Row $i$ is "where does token $i$'s query land?"
3. **Each output is a literal mixture** — Y[0] is some weighted blend of V[0], V[1], V[2]
   with weights that came from row 0 of A.

What multi-head attention adds (and what's NOT in this lesson): instead of one (Q, K, V)
triple, real models use 8–96 of them _in parallel_, each with its own learned matrices.
Each head can specialize on a different kind of relationship — one might track which
adjective modifies which noun, another might track which subject goes with which verb, and
so on. The outputs of all heads are concatenated and projected back down. Same mechanism,
applied many times. See the [multi-head attention lesson](/lessons/ml/multi-head-attention)
for the worked example.

</IntuitionOnly>

<MathOnly>

[Multi-head attention](/lessons/ml/multi-head-attention) is the natural next extension —
running $h$ heads in parallel with their own $W_Q^{(k)}, W_K^{(k)}, W_V^{(k)}$ matrices,
concatenating the per-head outputs, and projecting:

$$
\text{MHA}(X) = \text{concat}(Y^{(1)}, \ldots, Y^{(h)}) W_O
$$

See that lesson for the worked example with two heads, including hand-verifiable per-head
outputs. In real transformers the KQV matrices are low-rank decompositions:
$d_k = d_{\text{embed}} / h$ for $h$ heads, so the total parameter count is the same as a
single head with $d_k = d_{\text{embed}}$, just spread across more independent attention
computations. The remaining sections cover three things layered on top of the bare
mechanism: positional encoding, causal masking, and — for encoder-decoder models —
cross-attention.

</MathOnly>

## Why order matters: positional encoding

<PositionalEncodingViz />

<IntuitionOnly>

Stare at the attention computation above and you'll notice something weird: **it has no
notion of token order**. The matrix multiplications $QK^\top$ and $AV$ don't care which
row came first. If you shuffled the input tokens, attention would shuffle the output rows
in exactly the same way — but never produce a fundamentally different result.

That's a problem. "The cat sat on the mat" and "the mat sat on the cat" use the same
words. A language model has to tell them apart. Without something that injects position
into the embeddings, no amount of attention training will produce a model that knows
"subject before verb" vs "verb before subject."

The fix is comically simple: **add a position-dependent vector to every embedding before
sending it into Q, K, V**. The position vector is engineered (or learned) to be unique
per position, so once it's added, two tokens with identical embeddings but different
positions now have different inputs to the projections.

The viz shows the most common engineered choice — sinusoidal encoding. Every position
gets a vector built from sines and cosines at varying frequencies. Position 0 always maps
to $[\sin(0), \cos(0)] = [0, 1]$; position 1 to $[\sin(1), \cos(1)]$; and so on. The sin
and cos pair guarantees uniqueness, and the multi-frequency design (when $d_{\text{embed}}$
is larger than 2) gives the model a smooth scale of "how far apart are these two
positions" via the dot product of their PE vectors.

After the add, every row of $X^\prime = X + \text{PE}$ carries a unique position
fingerprint. The exact same attention math runs from there — but now the network has
something to grip when it needs to express "look at the token two before me."

</IntuitionOnly>

<MathOnly>

Vanilla attention is **permutation-equivariant**: for any permutation matrix $P$,
$\text{Attn}(P X) = P \cdot \text{Attn}(X)$. The output rows track the input rows. Two
sequences that differ only in token order produce outputs that differ only in row order
— and after collapsing them with the next layer's average or readout, they may become
indistinguishable. Order is invisible.

To break this, Vaswani et al. 2017 § 3.5 add a fixed sinusoidal positional encoding to
the embeddings before any projection:

$$
\text{PE}(pos, 2i)   = \sin\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i / d_{\text{model}}}}\right),
\quad
\text{PE}(pos, 2i+1) = \cos\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i / d_{\text{model}}}}\right)
$$

Then $X^\prime = X + \text{PE}$ feeds into Q, K, V. Two pleasant properties fall out of
the design:

- **Bounded.** Each PE component lies in $[-1, 1]$, so adding it doesn't dominate the
  embedding signal.
- **Linear position offsets.** For any fixed offset $k$, $\text{PE}(pos + k)$ is a linear
  function of $\text{PE}(pos)$ — the model can in principle learn to express
  "$k$ positions ahead" with a single fixed linear transformation, even at positions it
  never saw during training. (Whether attention layers in practice exploit this is
  another question; the property is at least available.)

The demo uses $d_{\text{embed}} = 2$, which collapses the formula to
$\text{PE}(pos) = [\sin(pos), \cos(pos)]$. For positions $0, 1, 2$:

| pos | PE                                       |
| --: | :--------------------------------------- |
|   0 | $[0, 1]$                                 |
|   1 | $[\sin 1, \cos 1] \approx [0.84, 0.54]$  |
|   2 | $[\sin 2, \cos 2] \approx [0.91, -0.42]$ |

Modern transformers replace fixed sinusoidal PE with learned positional embeddings or
rotary position embeddings (RoPE), but the high-level role is the same — give the
attention head something position-dependent to work with.

</MathOnly>

## Causal attention: predicting the next token

<CausalAttentionViz />

<IntuitionOnly>

When a language model is trained to predict the next token, it can't be allowed to
"cheat" by looking at later tokens. If position 3's job is to predict what comes at
position 4, then position 3's attention must only see positions $\leq 3$.

The trick is a **causal mask** applied to the score matrix right before softmax. Every
entry above the diagonal — every "this position is looking at a future position" entry —
gets set to $-\infty$. Since $e^{-\infty} = 0$, those positions contribute exactly zero
to the softmax sum, and the attention matrix ends up lower-triangular.

The viz uses the same fixed projection matrices and inputs as the plain attention demo,
but with `mask: "causal"`. Watch the mask step turn the upper triangle red, then watch
the softmax produce zeros where the mask was applied. **Row 0 of the attention matrix
ends up as $[1, 0, 0]$** — token 0 can only attend to itself, so its weight on itself is
forced to 1. The output $Y[0]$ therefore equals $V[0]$ exactly, no mixing.

This is what lets a transformer process the entire sequence in **one parallel forward
pass** during training (faster than walking the sequence token-by-token), while still
matching the autoregressive next-token-prediction objective. Train on all positions
simultaneously, just with this triangular mask preventing leakage from the future. At
inference time the model still generates one token at a time, but no separate code path
is needed — the mask makes the training-time forward pass behave the same way.

</IntuitionOnly>

<MathOnly>

The causal mask modifies attention as:

$$
S_{ij} \leftarrow \begin{cases} S_{ij} & j \leq i \\ -\infty & j > i \end{cases}
\quad\text{(applied to the scaled scores, just before softmax)}
$$

After row-wise softmax, $A_{ij} = 0$ for $j > i$ (because $e^{-\infty} = 0$ and the
remaining entries still normalize to 1 across the unmasked positions in the row). The
attention matrix is **lower-triangular**, and the output becomes:

$$
Y_i = \sum_{j=0}^{i} A_{ij} V_j
$$

— position $i$'s output is a convex combination of values $V_0, \ldots, V_i$ only. The
viz's row 0 illustrates the extreme case: $A_{0,*} = [1, 0, 0]$, so $Y_0 = V_0$ exactly,
no mixing.

**Why this preserves training efficiency:** without the mask, generating $n$ tokens
autoregressively requires $n$ separate forward passes (each one re-running attention on
the growing prefix). With the mask, a single forward pass over the full sequence
produces the next-token logits for **every** position simultaneously — the mask
guarantees position $i$'s logit only depends on positions $\leq i$, so the loss against
position $i+1$ is well-defined for every $i < n$. Training compute drops from
$\Theta(n^2)$ matmuls (one per position) to $\Theta(1)$ matmuls of size $n \times n$.

In production decoder-only LMs (GPT family, Llama, Claude's underlying architecture)
every attention layer is causal-masked. In encoder-decoder models (translation,
summarization), the encoder layers use the un-masked attention from the previous section
while the decoder layers use causal masking — plus a cross-attention sub-layer that
wires the two stacks together, which the next section covers.

</MathOnly>

## Cross-attention: letting the decoder read the encoder

<CrossAttentionViz />

<IntuitionOnly>

Every attention computation so far — plain and causal — has each token attend **within
its own sequence**. Cross-attention is the one place a transformer wires _two different
sequences_ together.

The classic setting is translation. An **encoder** reads the source sentence (say,
French) and turns it into a sequence of vectors — a fixed "memory" of what was said. A
**decoder** then generates the target sentence (English) one token at a time. Each time
the decoder emits a word, it has to look back at the French sentence to decide _which
source words matter right now_. "Which French word am I translating at this step?" is
exactly an attention query.

So cross-attention keeps the mechanism identical and changes only **where Q, K, and V
come from**:

- **Q comes from the decoder** — the sequence being generated asks the questions.
- **K and V come from the encoder** — the sequence being read offers the answers.

Because the two sequences can have different lengths, the attention matrix is no longer
square. The demo has a 2-token decoder and a 3-token encoder, so the attention matrix is
**2 × 3**: one row per decoder token, one column per encoder token. Row $d_1$ reads "how
much should decoder token 1 draw from each of the three encoder tokens?" — and like every
attention row, it sums to 1.

Step through and watch the source split: **Q** lights up in the decoder band, **K** and
**V** in the encoder band. Everything downstream — scores, scaling, softmax, weighted sum
— is the exact arithmetic from the first viz on this page. Only the inputs were rerouted.

Where it shows up: the original Transformer (the 2017 "Attention Is All You Need" paper
was a _translation_ model) and modern encoder-decoder models like T5 and BART.
Decoder-only LLMs — GPT, Llama, Claude's architecture — don't use cross-attention at all;
they're pure self-attention with causal masking. Cross-attention is the signature of an
encoder-decoder design.

</IntuitionOnly>

<MathOnly>

Cross-attention takes **two** input sequences instead of one: a decoder (target) sequence
$X_{\text{dec}} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d_{\text{embed}}}$ and an encoder (source)
sequence $X_{\text{enc}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{\text{embed}}}$. The query projection
reads the decoder; the key and value projections read the encoder:

$$
Q = X_{\text{dec}} W_Q \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d_k}, \quad
K = X_{\text{enc}} W_K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_k}, \quad
V = X_{\text{enc}} W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_v}
$$

$$
A = \text{softmax}\!\left( \frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n},
\quad Y = A V \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d_v}
$$

The score and attention matrices are **rectangular** — $m \times n$, not square — and the
output $Y$ has one row per decoder token. Plain self-attention is exactly the special case
$X_{\text{dec}} = X_{\text{enc}}$ (so $m = n$).

The demo uses $m = 2$ decoder tokens, $n = 3$ encoder tokens, and $d_k = d_v = 2$ with
$W_Q = I$, $W_K$ the dimension-swap, and $W_V = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$.
The raw scores $S = Q K^\top$ come out as integers:

$$
S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

After dividing by $\sqrt{2}$ and applying a row-wise softmax, the attention matrix is a
distribution over the three encoder tokens on each decoder row:

| A   |   e1 |   e2 |   e3 |
| :-- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| d1  | 0.14 | 0.28 | 0.58 |
| d2  | 0.45 | 0.11 | 0.45 |

and the output is $Y \approx \begin{bmatrix} 1.58 & -0.14 \\ 1.45 & 0.34 \end{bmatrix}$ —
one blended encoder value per decoder token.

**Where this sits in a real model.** An encoder-decoder transformer (the original Vaswani
et al. 2017 architecture, T5, BART) has three sub-layers per decoder block: _causal
self-attention_ (the decoder attends to its own past, from the previous section), then
_cross-attention_ (the decoder attends to the encoder memory computed once up front), then
a feed-forward layer. Decoder-only LLMs drop the encoder and the cross-attention sub-layer
entirely — every attention layer is causal self-attention.

</MathOnly>

## What's the point?

If you've followed [gradient descent](/lessons/ml/gradient-descent) and
[backpropagation](/lessons/ml/backprop), you now have the full training loop for a
transformer in your head:

1. Stack a few dozen attention + feed-forward layers.
2. Pass a batch of token sequences through; compute a next-token-prediction loss.
3. Backpropagate gradients through every layer — the recipe doesn't change.
4. Apply one gradient-descent step to every weight.
5. Repeat.

That's it. There's no other secret — just lots of compute and lots of data. The "magic"
of large language models is the scale, not the algorithm. The mechanism inside this lesson
is the same one running in every transformer ever shipped.
