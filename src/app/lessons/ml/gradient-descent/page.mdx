# Gradient Descent

**Difficulty:** beginner · **Category:** machine learning

<MathLevel />

<IntuitionOnly>

Imagine standing on a hillside in fog. You can feel which way is downhill under your feet, even
if you can't see the bottom. Take one small step downhill. Then do it again. Eventually you
reach the valley floor. That's **gradient descent** — the workhorse algorithm behind most of
modern machine learning.

The "hill" in this lesson is a simple bowl-shaped landscape: there's a single lowest point, and
every direction away from it goes up. You can see this geometry in the dashed rings on the
viz below — each ring is a contour at a fixed height, like an elevation line on a topographic
map. The lower the ring, the closer to the bottom.

Each step, the algorithm:

1. **Asks "which way is down?"** from the current point. (That's the gradient — though for now,
   just think of it as the steepest-downhill direction.)
2. **Walks a short distance** in that direction. How short? That's controlled by the **learning
   rate** — a small number you pick before the algorithm runs.

A learning rate that's too small wastes effort — tiny steps mean it takes forever to reach the
bottom. Too large and you can overshoot, bouncing back and forth across the valley instead of
settling in. The "0.32 (oscillates)" preset below makes that overshoot vivid.

</IntuitionOnly>

<MathOnly>

Given a differentiable loss $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ and a parameter vector
$\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, **gradient descent** iterates

$$
\mathbf{w}_{t+1} \leftarrow \mathbf{w}_t - \alpha \nabla L(\mathbf{w}_t)
$$

where $\alpha > 0$ is the **learning rate**. The gradient $\nabla L(\mathbf{w})$ points in the
direction of steepest _increase_, so subtracting it walks down the loss surface. The algorithm
terminates either when $\lVert \nabla L \rVert$ falls below a tolerance (we've reached a critical
point — for convex $L$, this is the global minimum) or when a fixed step budget is exhausted.

The toy loss used below is an asymmetric quadratic bowl:

$$
L(w_1, w_2) = w_1^2 + 3 w_2^2, \qquad \nabla L(w_1, w_2) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 w_1 \\ 6 w_2 \end{pmatrix}
$$

The dashed contour rings are level sets $L(w_1, w_2) = c$ — algebraically, ellipses with
semi-axes $\sqrt{c}$ along $w_1$ and $\sqrt{c/3}$ along $w_2$. Because the curvature is steeper
along $w_2$, the gradient is _six times_ more sensitive to $w_2$ than $w_1$. With a fixed step
size, the $w_2$ component overshoots before the $w_1$ component has made much progress — that's
the trajectory's bend.

The learning rate that makes the $w_2$ component oscillate is $\alpha > 1/3$: the multiplier
$|1 - 6\alpha|$ exceeds $1$ at $\alpha = 1/3$. The "0.32 (oscillates)" preset is right at the
edge.

</MathOnly>

<IntuitionOnly>

Try the four learning rates. The "cautious" one converges but takes a lot of steps. "Balanced"
and "brisk" both work well. "Oscillates" is past the safe limit — even though each step is
_pointed_ in the right direction, the step is too big and overshoots the valley floor.

What you can't see from the picture: every step requires computing the gradient. In the toy
2-parameter bowl, that's a tiny calculation. In a real neural network with millions of
parameters, computing the gradient is the expensive part of training — and a fast, accurate
gradient is what backpropagation provides (next lesson).

</IntuitionOnly>

<MathOnly>

A convergence guarantee for the bowl: with $\alpha < 1/3$, the iterate $\mathbf{w}_t$
contracts by a factor of $\max(|1 - 2\alpha|, |1 - 6\alpha|)$ per step. For $\alpha = 0.1$ that
multiplier is $\max(0.8, 0.4) = 0.8$, so $L(\mathbf{w}_t) \to 0$ at a geometric rate. For
$\alpha > 1/3$ the $w_2$-component multiplier exceeds $1$ — the iterate diverges along that
axis. (This is the simplest possible _convergence analysis_ of gradient descent: real losses
require Lipschitz conditions on $\nabla L$, but the geometry is the same.)

Gradient descent generalizes: same idea, same update, just with $\nabla L$ computed by
**backpropagation** through a network rather than by hand. That's the next lesson.

</MathOnly>

## Why "gradient" descent? Why not just "downhill"?

The gradient _is_ the downhill direction (locally, after flipping the sign) — but the word is
load-bearing in a few ways:

- **It's the steepest** downhill direction. Among all unit-length directions you could step in,
  $-\nabla L$ is the one that decreases $L$ the fastest.
- **It's local.** The gradient is computed at one specific point. If the landscape were not a
  smooth bowl but had a sharp ridge, the "downhill" direction could flip between adjacent
  points. Gradient descent still works, but you can no longer reason about each step as part of
  a single smooth path.
- **It scales with how steep the slope is.** A point near the rim of the bowl has a large
  gradient; a point near the bottom has a small one. So gradient descent automatically takes
  big steps where it's far from the minimum, and small steps as it converges — without any
  per-step learning-rate schedule.
